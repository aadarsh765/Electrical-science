01

Passive Circuit Components (R, L, C)

① Concept Breakdown

Passive components do not generate energy — they only store or dissipate it. There are three fundamental passive elements:

ComponentSymbolV–I RelationshipEnergy Behaviour
Resistor (R)Ω$v = iR$Dissipates as heat
Inductor (L)Henry (H)$v = L\dfrac{di}{dt}$Stores in magnetic field
Capacitor (C)Farad (F)$i = C\dfrac{dv}{dt}$Stores in electric field
💡 Analogy: Think of a resistor as friction in a pipe (resists flow), an inductor as a heavy flywheel (resists changes in current), and a capacitor as an elastic balloon (resists changes in voltage).

Key stored energy formulae:

  • Inductor: $\displaystyle W_L = \frac{1}{2}LI^2$
  • Capacitor: $\displaystyle W_C = \frac{1}{2}CV^2$
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Differentiate between resistors, inductors, and capacitors in terms of their V–I relationship and energy storage behaviour.
THEORETICAL Q2
Why is an inductor called a "current memory" device and a capacitor a "voltage memory" device?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: A 200 mH inductor carries a current that ramps linearly from 0 A to 5 A in 2 ms. Find the voltage across it and the energy stored at $t = 2$ ms.

STEP 1 — Find di/dt
$$\frac{di}{dt} = \frac{5 - 0}{2 \times 10^{-3}} = 2500 \text{ A/s}$$
STEP 2 — Inductor voltage
$$v = L\frac{di}{dt} = 200 \times 10^{-3} \times 2500 = 500 \text{ V}$$
STEP 3 — Stored energy
$$W_L = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.2)(5)^2 = 2.5 \text{ J}$$
✓ v = 500 V, W_L = 2.5 J
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Confusing the units: Farads, Henrys, Ohms — always carry units in every step.
  • For capacitors, forgetting that current is $C\,dv/dt$, not $v/C$.
  • Mixing up "energy stored" vs "power dissipated" — inductors and capacitors store, resistors dissipate.
02

Basic Laws of Electrical Engineering (Ohm's Law)

① Concept Breakdown

Ohm's Law is the cornerstone: at constant temperature, the voltage across a conductor is directly proportional to the current through it.

$$\boxed{V = IR}$$

Rearranged forms used frequently in exams:

  • $I = V/R$ (current from voltage)
  • $R = V/I$ (resistance from measurements)
  • Power: $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$

Conductance $G = 1/R$ (in Siemens, S) is sometimes used in nodal analysis.

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State Ohm's Law and explain its limitations. Under what conditions does it fail?
THEORETICAL Q2
A 100 Ω resistor dissipates 40 W. Find the current through it and the voltage across it.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: A resistor dissipates 40 W when connected to a 100 V supply. Find R and I.

STEP 1 — Find R from P = V²/R
$$R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{100^2}{40} = 250 \;\Omega$$
STEP 2 — Find I
$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{100}{250} = 0.4 \text{ A}$$
✓ R = 250 Ω, I = 0.4 A
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Ohm's Law does NOT apply to non-linear devices (diodes, transistors).
  • Using $P = V^2/R$ when V is RMS — ensure consistency between peak and RMS values in AC contexts.
03

Temperature Resistance Coefficients

① Concept Breakdown

Resistance changes with temperature. This is characterized by the Temperature Coefficient of Resistance (α):

$$\boxed{R_T = R_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right]}$$

Where $R_0$ is resistance at reference temperature $T_0$ (usually 20°C or 0°C), and $\alpha$ is in per °C (or per K).

Materialα (per °C at 20°C)Type
Copper+0.00393Positive (PTC)
Aluminum+0.00403Positive (PTC)
Nichrome+0.00017Positive (PTC)
Carbon−0.0005Negative (NTC)
Silicon (semiconductor)NegativeNTC
💡 Analogy: In metals, higher temperature = more atomic vibrations = more collisions for electrons = higher resistance. In semiconductors, higher temperature = more free carriers = lower resistance.
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Distinguish between Positive Temperature Coefficient (PTC) and Negative Temperature Coefficient (NTC) materials with examples.
THEORETICAL Q2
Explain why semiconductor resistance decreases with temperature while metallic resistance increases.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: A copper coil has resistance 50 Ω at 20°C. Find resistance at 80°C. ($\alpha_{Cu} = 0.00393$/°C)

STEP 1 — Apply formula
$$R_{80} = R_{20}\left[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)\right] = 50\left[1 + 0.00393(80-20)\right]$$
STEP 2 — Compute
$$R_{80} = 50\left[1 + 0.2358\right] = 50 \times 1.2358 = 61.79 \;\Omega$$
✓ R₈₀ ≈ 61.8 Ω
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Not checking the reference temperature ($T_0$) — it may be 0°C or 20°C depending on the problem.
  • Using the temperature difference $(T - T_0)$, not just $T$.

Voltage and Current Sources

① Concept Breakdown
PropertyIdeal Voltage SourceIdeal Current Source
MaintainsConstant terminal voltage regardless of currentConstant current regardless of voltage
Internal resistance$r_s = 0$$r_s = \infty$
Short circuitInfinite current (ideal)Defines the short-circuit current
Open circuitDefines terminal voltageInfinite voltage across terminals

Practical sources are modelled with an internal resistance $r_s$:

  • Practical voltage source: ideal $V_s$ in series with $r_s$. Terminal voltage: $V_T = V_s - I \cdot r_s$
  • Practical current source: ideal $I_s$ in parallel with $r_s$.

Source Transformation: A voltage source $V_s$ in series with $R$ can be converted to a current source $I_s = V_s/R$ in parallel with the same $R$.

$$\boxed{I_s = \frac{V_s}{R}, \quad V_s = I_s \cdot R}$$
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Differentiate between ideal and practical voltage sources. How does loading affect terminal voltage?
THEORETICAL Q2
Explain source transformation with a diagram. When is it useful in circuit analysis?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Convert a 12 V source with 4 Ω internal resistance into an equivalent current source.

STEP 1
$$I_s = \frac{V_s}{r_s} = \frac{12}{4} = 3 \text{ A}$$
STEP 2
Place $I_s = 3$ A current source in parallel with $r_s = 4\;\Omega$.
✓ Equivalent: 3 A current source ∥ 4 Ω
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • In source transformation, the resistor switches from series (voltage source) to parallel (current source).
  • Dependent sources cannot be deactivated during Thevenin/Norton analysis — they stay active.
05

Series and Parallel Circuits, Power & Energy

① Concept Breakdown
PropertySeriesParallel
CurrentSame through all: $I_1 = I_2 = I$Same voltage: $V_1 = V_2 = V$
Voltage$V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots$$I = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots$
Resistance$R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots$$\dfrac{1}{R_{eq}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2} + \cdots$
Two resistors parallel—$R_{eq} = \dfrac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$

Voltage Divider (series): $\displaystyle V_x = V_s \cdot \frac{R_x}{R_{total}}$

Current Divider (parallel, 2 resistors): $\displaystyle I_1 = I_s \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$

Power dissipated: $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$. Energy: $W = P \times t$ (in Joules).

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
A 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistor are in parallel. Derive the equivalent resistance and explain the current division principle.
THEORETICAL Q2
Why do household appliances connect in parallel rather than series?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Three resistors R₁ = 6 Ω, R₂ = 3 Ω, R₃ = 2 Ω. R₁ is in series with the parallel combination of R₂ and R₃. Source voltage = 12 V. Find total current and power delivered by source.

STEP 1 — Parallel combo R₂ ∥ R₃
$$R_{23} = \frac{3 \times 2}{3+2} = \frac{6}{5} = 1.2\;\Omega$$
STEP 2 — Total resistance
$$R_{eq} = R_1 + R_{23} = 6 + 1.2 = 7.2\;\Omega$$
STEP 3 — Total current
$$I = \frac{V}{R_{eq}} = \frac{12}{7.2} = 1.667\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — Power
$$P = VI = 12 \times 1.667 = 20\text{ W}$$
✓ I = 1.667 A, P = 20 W
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • For current divider with two resistors: $I_1 = I_s \cdot R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ — note it's the other resistor in the numerator.
  • When calculating $R_{eq}$ for n parallel resistors, don't forget to take the reciprocal of the sum of reciprocals.
06

Kirchhoff's Laws (KVL & KCL)

① Concept Breakdown

KCL — Kirchhoff's Current Law: At any node, the algebraic sum of all currents equals zero (conservation of charge).

$$\boxed{\sum I_{in} = \sum I_{out} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum I = 0}$$
💡 Analogy: Water flowing into a pipe junction must equal water flowing out. No charge accumulates at a node.

KVL — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law: Around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and drops equals zero (conservation of energy).

$$\boxed{\sum V = 0 \quad \text{(around any closed loop)}}$$
💡 Analogy: If you walk around a hill and return to the start, your net elevation change is zero — regardless of the path taken.

Sign convention for KVL: Choose a direction (CW or CCW). Voltage rise (+) when traversing from − to + terminal; voltage drop (−) when traversing + to −.

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State KCL and KVL. On what fundamental physical principles are they based?
THEORETICAL Q2
How many independent KCL and KVL equations can be written for a circuit with $N$ nodes and $B$ branches?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: In a single loop: 20 V source, R₁ = 4 Ω, R₂ = 6 Ω in series. Find current I and voltage across each resistor.

STEP 1 — Apply KVL (clockwise loop)
$$20 - 4I - 6I = 0 \implies 10I = 20 \implies I = 2\text{ A}$$
STEP 2 — Voltage drops
$$V_{R_1} = 4 \times 2 = 8\text{ V}, \quad V_{R_2} = 6 \times 2 = 12\text{ V}$$
STEP 3 — Verify KVL
$$20 - 8 - 12 = 0 \;\checkmark$$
✓ I = 2 A, V_R1 = 8 V, V_R2 = 12 V
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Inconsistent sign convention in KVL — pick one direction and stick to it throughout the loop.
  • Number of independent KCL equations = N−1 (not N). The last node equation is redundant.
  • KVL applies to any loop, even if no source is present.
07

Nodal & Mesh Analysis

① Concept Breakdown

Nodal Analysis uses KCL at each non-reference node. Unknowns = node voltages $V_1, V_2, \ldots$

  • Choose a reference (ground) node.
  • At each node, $\sum$currents leaving = 0: $\dfrac{V_n - V_k}{R} = $ source current.
  • Solve the resulting system of equations.

Mesh Analysis uses KVL around each mesh (independent loop). Unknowns = mesh currents $I_1, I_2, \ldots$

  • Assign clockwise mesh currents.
  • Apply KVL: $\sum V = 0$ for each mesh.
  • For shared branches: current = $I_m - I_n$.

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Compare nodal and mesh analysis. When would you prefer one over the other?
THEORETICAL Q2
What is a "supermesh"? When does it arise and how is it handled?
③ Numerical Problem — Nodal

Problem: Circuit: Node V₁ connected to 10 V source (ground on other side), 5 Ω to ground, and 10 Ω between V₁ and V₂. Node V₂ connected to 2 A source (into node) and 4 Ω to ground. Find V₁ and V₂.

STEP 1 — Node V₁: V₁ is fixed by voltage source
$$V_1 = 10\text{ V}$$
STEP 2 — KCL at Node V₂
$$\frac{V_2 - V_1}{10} + \frac{V_2}{4} = 2$$
STEP 3 — Substitute V₁ = 10
$$\frac{V_2 - 10}{10} + \frac{V_2}{4} = 2 \implies 0.1V_2 - 1 + 0.25V_2 = 2$$ $$0.35V_2 = 3 \implies V_2 = 8.57\text{ V}$$
✓ V₁ = 10 V, V₂ ≈ 8.57 V
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • In nodal analysis, forgetting to subtract when current flows from $V_n$ to $V_k$: it's $(V_n - V_k)/R$, not $V_n/R$.
  • In mesh analysis, for a shared branch between mesh 1 and 2: current through it is $(I_1 - I_2)$, not just $I_1$.
  • Not handling supernodes (when a voltage source connects two non-reference nodes) — write a constraint equation alongside KCL.
08

Delta–Star (Δ–Y) Transformation

① Concept Breakdown

Some circuits cannot be reduced using series/parallel rules alone. The Δ–Y transformation converts between two equivalent three-terminal configurations.

Delta (Δ) → Star (Y): Each star resistor = (product of adjacent delta resistors) ÷ (sum of all delta resistors).

$$\boxed{R_a = \frac{R_{ab}\cdot R_{ca}}{R_{ab}+R_{bc}+R_{ca}}, \quad R_b = \frac{R_{ab}\cdot R_{bc}}{R_{ab}+R_{bc}+R_{ca}}, \quad R_c = \frac{R_{bc}\cdot R_{ca}}{R_{ab}+R_{bc}+R_{ca}}}$$

Star (Y) → Delta (Δ):

$$\boxed{R_{ab} = R_a + R_b + \frac{R_a R_b}{R_c}, \quad \text{(and cyclically for } R_{bc}, R_{ca}\text{)}}$$

For a balanced network where all Δ resistors = $R_\Delta$: $R_Y = R_\Delta / 3$. Conversely, $R_\Delta = 3R_Y$.

💡 Memory aid: Δ→Y: each star arm = product of two touching Δ arms ÷ sum of all three. Think "shrink inward."
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Why is delta-star transformation necessary? Illustrate with a circuit that cannot be solved by simple series/parallel reduction.
THEORETICAL Q2
For a balanced delta network with each arm = 30 Ω, what is the equivalent star resistance?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Δ network: $R_{AB} = 30\;\Omega$, $R_{BC} = 60\;\Omega$, $R_{CA} = 90\;\Omega$. Find equivalent Y resistors.

STEP 1 — Sum
$$\Sigma = 30 + 60 + 90 = 180\;\Omega$$
STEP 2 — Star resistors
$$R_A = \frac{R_{AB}\cdot R_{CA}}{\Sigma} = \frac{30\times90}{180} = 15\;\Omega$$ $$R_B = \frac{R_{AB}\cdot R_{BC}}{\Sigma} = \frac{30\times60}{180} = 10\;\Omega$$ $$R_C = \frac{R_{BC}\cdot R_{CA}}{\Sigma} = \frac{60\times90}{180} = 30\;\Omega$$
✓ R_A = 15 Ω, R_B = 10 Ω, R_C = 30 Ω
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Confusing the Y→Δ and Δ→Y formulas — memorize one and derive the other.
  • Always label nodes clearly (A, B, C) before applying formulas — mislabelling is the #1 error.
09

Superposition Theorem

① Concept Breakdown

In a linear circuit with multiple sources, the response (voltage or current) at any branch equals the algebraic sum of responses due to each independent source acting alone (others deactivated).

  • Deactivate a voltage source: replace with a short circuit.
  • Deactivate a current source: replace with an open circuit.
💡 Analogy: The noise in a room from two speakers independently adds up. Superposition lets you solve each "speaker" alone, then add results.

Important: Superposition applies to voltage and current, NOT directly to power (since power is non-linear: $P \propto I^2$).

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State the Superposition Theorem. Why does it not apply directly to power calculations?
THEORETICAL Q2
Can superposition be applied to circuits with dependent sources? Explain.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Circuit: 6 V source and 3 A current source, with R₁ = 2 Ω in series with parallel combo of R₂ = 4 Ω. Find current through R₂ using superposition.

CASE 1 — Only 6 V source (open-circuit the 3 A source)
$$I' = \frac{6}{2 + 4} = 1\text{ A (through R₂)}$$
CASE 2 — Only 3 A source (short-circuit the 6 V source)
Using current divider (R₁ ∥ ... — assume R₁ and R₂ in parallel from source perspective): $$I'' = 3 \times \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} = 3 \times \frac{2}{2+4} = 1\text{ A}$$
STEP 3 — Superpose (check directions, assume additive)
$$I_{R_2} = I' + I'' = 1 + 1 = 2\text{ A}$$
✓ I_R2 = 2 A
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Forgetting to check the direction of each partial current before adding — they may subtract.
  • Dependent sources are NEVER deactivated — only independent sources are turned off.
  • Superposition cannot be used to find power directly.
10

Thevenin's Theorem

① Concept Breakdown

Any linear two-terminal circuit can be replaced by a single voltage source $V_{th}$ in series with a single resistance $R_{th}$.

  • $V_{th}$ = Open-circuit voltage at the terminals (remove the load, measure $V_{OC}$).
  • $R_{th}$ = Equivalent resistance seen from the terminals with all independent sources deactivated.
$$\boxed{V_{th} = V_{OC}, \quad R_{th} = R_{eq}\big|_{\text{sources deactivated}}}$$
💡 Analogy: No matter how complex your power grid, from your socket's perspective, it's just one voltage source with an internal resistance.

With load $R_L$ connected: $\displaystyle I_L = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th} + R_L}$

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State Thevenin's theorem. What is the procedure to find $V_{th}$ and $R_{th}$?
THEORETICAL Q2
How do you find $R_{th}$ when the circuit contains dependent sources?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: A circuit with 12 V source, R₁ = 6 Ω in series, R₂ = 4 Ω in parallel with the output terminals AB. Find Thevenin equivalent and current through $R_L = 2\;\Omega$.

STEP 1 — Find V_th (open circuit AB, no current in R₂... wait, R₂ connects from series node to B)
By voltage divider: $$V_{th} = 12 \times \frac{4}{6+4} = 4.8\text{ V}$$
STEP 2 — Find R_th (short 12 V source)
$$R_{th} = R_1 \| R_2 = \frac{6 \times 4}{6+4} = 2.4\;\Omega$$
STEP 3 — Load current
$$I_L = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th} + R_L} = \frac{4.8}{2.4+2} = \frac{4.8}{4.4} \approx 1.09\text{ A}$$
✓ V_th = 4.8 V, R_th = 2.4 Ω, I_L ≈ 1.09 A
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Removing the load before finding $V_{OC}$ is mandatory — students often forget to open-circuit the terminals.
  • With dependent sources: deactivate only independent sources; inject a test voltage $V_T$ (or current $I_T$) and find $R_{th} = V_T/I_T$.
11

Norton's Theorem

① Concept Breakdown

The dual of Thevenin's theorem: any linear two-terminal circuit = a current source $I_N$ in parallel with resistance $R_N$.

$$\boxed{I_N = I_{SC} = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th}}, \quad R_N = R_{th}}$$

$I_N$ = short-circuit current at terminals (place wire across AB, measure current).

$R_N$ = same as $R_{th}$ (deactivate sources, find equivalent resistance).

TheveninNorton
Circuit$V_{th}$ in series with $R_{th}$$I_N$ in parallel with $R_N$
Key quantityOpen-circuit voltageShort-circuit current
Relationship$V_{th} = I_N \cdot R_N$
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State Norton's theorem and show that it is the dual of Thevenin's theorem.
THEORETICAL Q2
Convert the Thevenin equivalent ($V_{th}$ = 8 V, $R_{th}$ = 4 Ω) to Norton form.
③ Numerical

Using previous Thevenin result ($V_{th} = 4.8$ V, $R_{th} = 2.4$ Ω), find Norton equivalent:

Norton current
$$I_N = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th}} = \frac{4.8}{2.4} = 2\text{ A}$$
Norton resistance
$$R_N = R_{th} = 2.4\;\Omega$$
✓ I_N = 2 A ∥ R_N = 2.4 Ω
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Measuring $I_{SC}$ while the circuit is loaded — terminals must be shorted (not left open).
  • $R_N = R_{th}$ always — they are the same equivalent resistance.
12

Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

① Concept Breakdown

Maximum power is delivered to a load when the load resistance equals the source's Thevenin resistance:

$$\boxed{R_L = R_{th} \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{max} = \frac{V_{th}^2}{4R_{th}}}$$

At this condition:

  • Efficiency = 50% (half the power is lost in $R_{th}$).
  • Load current: $I_L = V_{th}/(2R_{th})$
💡 Analogy: Like matching a speaker's impedance to an amplifier — when matched, you get maximum sound (power) transfer. Speaker manufacturers design their speakers at specific impedance (8Ω, 4Ω) for this reason.
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
State and prove the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem using calculus (differentiate $P_L$ w.r.t. $R_L$ and set to zero).
THEORETICAL Q2
Why is the efficiency only 50% at maximum power transfer? Is this desirable in power systems?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: A source has $V_{th} = 12$ V and $R_{th} = 3\;\Omega$. Find (a) $R_L$ for MPT, (b) $P_{max}$.

STEP 1
$$R_L = R_{th} = 3\;\Omega$$
STEP 2
$$P_{max} = \frac{V_{th}^2}{4R_{th}} = \frac{144}{12} = 12\text{ W}$$
✓ R_L = 3 Ω, P_max = 12 W
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Using $P = V_{th}^2/R_{th}$ — the correct formula is $V_{th}^2/(4R_{th})$, accounting for the voltage divider.
  • Confusing MPT (used in communication/electronics) with efficiency goals (used in power systems, where efficiency is paramount).
13

Time Domain Analysis: First-Order RC & RL Circuits

① Concept Breakdown

First-order circuits have one energy storage element (C or L) and respond to a step input with an exponential transient.

General form of response:

$$\boxed{x(t) = x(\infty) + \left[x(0^+) - x(\infty)\right]e^{-t/\tau}}$$

where $x(t)$ is voltage or current, $x(0^+)$ is initial value, $x(\infty)$ is final (steady-state) value, and $\tau$ is the time constant.

RC CircuitRL Circuit
Time constant$\tau = RC$$\tau = L/R$
Charging quantityCapacitor voltage $v_C$Inductor current $i_L$
At $t = \tau$Response reaches ~63.2% of final value
Fully settled~5τ (99.3% of final value)

RC Charging: $v_C(t) = V_s\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)$ for initially uncharged capacitor.

RL Energizing: $i_L(t) = \dfrac{V_s}{R}\left(1 - e^{-Rt/L}\right)$

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Derive the expression for voltage across a capacitor in an RC circuit energized by a DC voltage step $V_s$.
THEORETICAL Q2
What is meant by "time constant"? What is its physical significance for RC and RL circuits?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: RC series circuit: $V_s = 100$ V, $R = 10$ kΩ, $C = 50$ μF, $v_C(0) = 0$. Find (a) τ, (b) $v_C$ at $t = \tau$, (c) $v_C$ at $t = 200$ ms.

STEP 1 — Time constant
$$\tau = RC = 10\times10^3 \times 50\times10^{-6} = 0.5\text{ s} = 500\text{ ms}$$
STEP 2 — At t = τ
$$v_C(\tau) = 100(1 - e^{-1}) = 100(1 - 0.368) = 63.2\text{ V}$$
STEP 3 — At t = 200 ms
$$v_C(0.2) = 100\left(1 - e^{-0.2/0.5}\right) = 100(1-e^{-0.4}) = 100(1-0.6703) = 32.97\text{ V}$$
✓ τ = 500 ms, v_C(τ) = 63.2 V, v_C(200ms) ≈ 33 V
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • $\tau = RC$ (not $R/C$ or $1/RC$); $\tau = L/R$ (not $LR$) — keep units consistent (seconds).
  • Initial condition $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$ (capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously); $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$ (inductor current cannot change instantaneously).
  • Final value at $t \to \infty$: capacitor is open circuit (no current), inductor is short circuit (no voltage).

Representation of Sinusoidal Waveforms — Peak & RMS Values

① Concept Breakdown

The standard sinusoidal voltage:

$$\boxed{v(t) = V_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)}$$

where $V_m$ = peak (amplitude), $\omega = 2\pi f$ = angular frequency (rad/s), $\phi$ = phase angle.

Phasor Representation

① Concept Breakdown

A phasor is a complex number representation of a sinusoidal quantity. It captures magnitude and phase, eliminating the time variable.

$$v(t) = V_m\sin(\omega t + \phi) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \mathbf{V} = V_{rms}\angle\phi \;=\; V_{rms}(\cos\phi + j\sin\phi)$$

Phasors use RMS values by convention (in power calculations).

Impedance generalizes resistance to AC:

ElementImpedance ZPhase shift
Resistor R$Z_R = R$V and I in phase (0°)
Inductor L$Z_L = j\omega L = jX_L$V leads I by 90°
Capacitor C$Z_C = 1/(j\omega C) = -jX_C$I leads V by 90°

Ohm's Law in phasor domain: $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Z} \cdot \mathbf{I}$

💡 Memory trick: "ELI the ICE man" — in an L: E (voltage) leads I; in a C: I leads E.
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
What is a phasor? How does phasor representation simplify AC circuit analysis?
THEORETICAL Q2
Define inductive reactance $X_L$ and capacitive reactance $X_C$. How do they depend on frequency?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: $v(t) = 100\sin(100\pi t + 45°)$ V applied to a 0.1 H inductor. Find $X_L$, phasor current, and $i(t)$.

STEP 1 — ω and X_L
$$\omega = 100\pi \approx 314\text{ rad/s}, \quad X_L = \omega L = 314 \times 0.1 = 31.4\;\Omega$$
STEP 2 — Phasor voltage (using peak or RMS; use RMS)
$$\mathbf{V} = \frac{100}{\sqrt{2}}\angle 45° = 70.71\angle 45°\text{ V}$$
STEP 3 — Phasor current
$$\mathbf{I} = \frac{\mathbf{V}}{Z_L} = \frac{70.71\angle 45°}{31.4\angle 90°} = 2.25\angle{-45°}\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — Time domain
$$i(t) = 2.25\sqrt{2}\sin(100\pi t - 45°) = 3.18\sin(100\pi t - 45°)\text{ A}$$
✓ X_L = 31.4 Ω, I_m = 3.18 A, lagging V by 90°
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • $Z_C = -jX_C = 1/(j\omega C)$ — the negative sign matters for phase calculations.
  • Mixing peak and RMS in phasor analysis — be consistent throughout a problem.
  • $X_L$ increases with frequency; $X_C$ decreases with frequency.

AC Power: Real, Reactive, Apparent & Power Factor

① Concept Breakdown

In AC circuits, power takes three forms:

TypeSymbolFormulaUnitMeaning
Real (Active)$P$$V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\phi$Watts (W)Actual power consumed
Reactive$Q$$V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\phi$VARPower stored/released by L & C
Apparent$S$$V_{rms}I_{rms} = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2}$VATotal power supplied by source

Power Triangle: $S^2 = P^2 + Q^2$

$$\boxed{\cos\phi = \frac{P}{S} = \text{Power Factor (PF)}}$$

PF = 1 (resistive load, ideal); PF < 1 (reactive load). Lagging PF: inductive load ($\phi > 0$). Leading PF: capacitive load ($\phi < 0$).

💡 Analogy: In a beer mug: $P$ = actual beer drunk, $Q$ = useless foam, $S$ = total mug content. PF = beer/(beer + foam).
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Define power factor. Explain the significance of a low power factor in an industrial setting. How is power factor improvement achieved?
THEORETICAL Q2
An inductive load draws 10 A at 0.8 pf lagging from a 230 V supply. Find P, Q, and S.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Load: 5 kW at 0.707 lagging pf, 230 V supply. Find (a) $S$, (b) $Q$, (c) current drawn, (d) capacitor required to improve pf to unity at 50 Hz.

STEP 1 — Apparent power
$$S = \frac{P}{\cos\phi} = \frac{5000}{0.707} = 7071\text{ VA} = 7.07\text{ kVA}$$
STEP 2 — Reactive power ($\phi = 45°$ since $\cos 45° = 0.707$)
$$Q = S\sin\phi = 7071 \times \sin 45° = 5000\text{ VAR} = 5\text{ kVAR}$$
STEP 3 — Current
$$I = \frac{S}{V} = \frac{7071}{230} = 30.74\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — Capacitor for pf correction to unity
$$Q_C = Q = 5000\text{ VAR}, \quad C = \frac{Q_C}{\omega V^2} = \frac{5000}{2\pi \times 50 \times 230^2} = 300.6\;\mu\text{F}$$
✓ S = 7.07 kVA, Q = 5 kVAR, I = 30.74 A, C ≈ 300 μF
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Using peak values instead of RMS in power formulas — power formulas always use RMS.
  • Saying "capacitive load has lagging pf" — it's the opposite: capacitive → leading, inductive → lagging.
  • $Q$ for inductor is positive; for capacitor is negative — careful with sign when adding.

Single-Phase AC Analysis: R, L, C, RL, RC, RLC (Series & Parallel)

① Concept Breakdown

Series RLC:

$$Z = R + j(X_L - X_C) = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$ $$|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \quad \phi = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{X_L - X_C}{R}\right)$$
Condition$X_L$ vs $X_C$Circuit behaviourPF
$X_L > X_C$InductiveV leads ILagging
$X_L < X_C$CapacitiveI leads VLeading
$X_L = X_C$ResonanceZ = R (minimum)Unity

Parallel RLC: Use admittance $Y = 1/Z$:

$$Y = G + j(B_C - B_L) = \frac{1}{R} + j\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right)$$

where $G = 1/R$ (conductance), $B_L = 1/X_L$ (inductive susceptance), $B_C = 1/X_C$ (capacitive susceptance).

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Draw the impedance triangle for a series RLC circuit and define impedance, reactance, and phase angle.
THEORETICAL Q2
Compare series and parallel RLC circuits in terms of impedance and resonance conditions.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Series RLC: $R = 10\;\Omega$, $L = 50$ mH, $C = 200\;\mu$F, supply $v = 100\sin(200t)$. Find Z, I, and PF.

STEP 1 — ω = 200 rad/s
$$X_L = \omega L = 200 \times 0.05 = 10\;\Omega$$ $$X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{200 \times 200\times10^{-6}} = 25\;\Omega$$
STEP 2 — Impedance
$$Z = R + j(X_L - X_C) = 10 + j(10-25) = 10 - j15\;\Omega$$ $$|Z| = \sqrt{100 + 225} = \sqrt{325} \approx 18.03\;\Omega$$
STEP 3 — Current (peak)
$$I_m = \frac{V_m}{|Z|} = \frac{100}{18.03} = 5.55\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — Phase and PF
$$\phi = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-15}{10}\right) = -56.3° \quad\text{(capacitive/leading)}$$ $$\text{PF} = \cos(56.3°) = 0.555\text{ leading}$$
✓ |Z| = 18.03 Ω, I_m = 5.55 A, PF = 0.555 leading
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Net reactance = $X_L - X_C$ (not $X_L + X_C$) — they oppose each other.
  • For parallel circuits, work with admittance ($Y = 1/Z$), not impedance — don't add impedances directly in parallel.
  • Phase angle sign: positive $\phi$ = inductive (lagging), negative $\phi$ = capacitive (leading).

Resonance (Series & Parallel)

① Concept Breakdown

Resonance occurs when inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, leaving a purely resistive impedance.

Resonant frequency:

$$\boxed{f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \quad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}}$$
PropertySeries ResonanceParallel Resonance
Impedance at $f_0$$Z = R$ (minimum)$Z = L/RC$ (maximum)
Current at $f_0$MaximumMinimum
Quality Factor Q$Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 CR}$$Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$
Bandwidth BW$BW = f_0/Q = R/(2\pi L)$$BW = f_0/Q$

At resonance in series RLC: $V_L = V_C = QV_s$ (voltage magnification). This can be very large!

💡 Analogy: A swing resonates at its natural frequency — small pushes create huge amplitude. Similarly, at resonance, small voltage can produce very large $V_L$ and $V_C$.
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
Define resonance in a series RLC circuit. Derive the expression for resonant frequency.
THEORETICAL Q2
Define Q factor and bandwidth. What is the significance of a high Q factor in a tuning circuit?
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Series RLC: R = 5 Ω, L = 0.1 H, C = 10 μF. Find (a) $f_0$, (b) Q, (c) bandwidth BW.

STEP 1 — Resonant frequency
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{0.1 \times 10\times10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 10^{-3}} \approx 159.15\text{ Hz}$$
STEP 2 — Q factor
$$Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{2\pi \times 159.15 \times 0.1}{5} = \frac{100}{5} = 20$$
STEP 3 — Bandwidth
$$BW = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{159.15}{20} = 7.96\text{ Hz}$$
✓ f₀ ≈ 159 Hz, Q = 20, BW ≈ 7.96 Hz
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Series resonance → minimum impedance (maximum current); parallel resonance → maximum impedance (minimum current). Students often flip these.
  • Voltage across L and C at series resonance = $Q \times V_s$, which can be dangerously large for high-Q circuits.
  • $f_0$ formula is the same for both series and parallel — but behaviour differs drastically.

Three-Phase Balanced Circuits

① Concept Breakdown

Three-phase systems use three sinusoidal voltages, 120° apart:

$$v_a = V_m\sin(\omega t), \quad v_b = V_m\sin(\omega t - 120°), \quad v_c = V_m\sin(\omega t - 240°)$$

For a balanced system: all three phase voltages are equal in magnitude and 120° apart. The phasor sum = 0.

Advantages over single-phase:

  • Constant power delivery (no pulsation)
  • More efficient power transmission
  • Self-starting rotating magnetic fields for motors
  • 25% less conductor material needed than equivalent single-phase

Balanced 3-phase power:

$$\boxed{P_{3\phi} = \sqrt{3}\,V_L I_L \cos\phi, \quad Q_{3\phi} = \sqrt{3}\,V_L I_L \sin\phi, \quad S_{3\phi} = \sqrt{3}\,V_L I_L}$$
② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1
What is a balanced three-phase system? Show that the sum of three balanced phase voltages is zero.
THEORETICAL Q2
A balanced 3-phase load draws 30 kW at 0.8 pf lagging from a 400 V (line) supply. Find line current.
③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Balanced star-connected load: $Z = 10 + j6\;\Omega$ per phase, line voltage = 415 V. Find (a) phase voltage, (b) phase current, (c) total power.

STEP 1 — Phase voltage (star)
$$V_{ph} = \frac{V_L}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{415}{1.732} = 239.6\text{ V}$$
STEP 2 — Impedance magnitude
$$|Z| = \sqrt{10^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{136} = 11.66\;\Omega$$
STEP 3 — Phase current (= line current in star)
$$I_{ph} = I_L = \frac{V_{ph}}{|Z|} = \frac{239.6}{11.66} = 20.55\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — PF and power
$$\cos\phi = \frac{R}{|Z|} = \frac{10}{11.66} = 0.858$$ $$P = \sqrt{3}V_L I_L\cos\phi = 1.732 \times 415 \times 20.55 \times 0.858 = 12.65\text{ kW}$$
✓ V_ph = 239.6 V, I_L = 20.55 A, P ≈ 12.65 kW
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • Forgetting the $\sqrt{3}$ factor when converting between line and phase quantities.
  • The 3-phase power formula uses line voltage $V_L$ and line current $I_L$, always — not phase values.
  • In a balanced system, neutral current = 0 (no need for a neutral wire carrying current).

② Examiner's Perspective
THEORETICAL Q1

THEORETICAL Q2

③ Numerical Problem

Problem: Delta-connected balanced load: Z = 30∠36.87° Ω per phase, line voltage = 400 V. Find line current and total power.

STEP 1 — Phase voltage (delta: V_ph = V_L)
$$V_{ph} = V_L = 400\text{ V}$$
STEP 2 — Phase current
$$I_{ph} = \frac{V_{ph}}{|Z|} = \frac{400}{30} = 13.33\text{ A}$$
STEP 3 — Line current
$$I_L = \sqrt{3}\,I_{ph} = 1.732 \times 13.33 = 23.09\text{ A}$$
STEP 4 — Power ($\phi = 36.87°$, $\cos\phi = 0.8$)
$$P = \sqrt{3} \times 400 \times 23.09 \times 0.8 = 12.8\text{ kW}$$
✓ I_ph = 13.33 A, I_L = 23.09 A, P = 12.8 kW
⚠ Key Pitfalls
  • In delta: $V_{ph} = V_L$ (NOT divided by $\sqrt{3}$) — the most common exam error.
  • In star: $I_{ph} = I_L$ (NOT multiplied by $\sqrt{3}$).
  • Always specify whether given voltage is line or phase, and whether current is line or phase.
